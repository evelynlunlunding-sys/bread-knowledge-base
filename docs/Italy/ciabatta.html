Ciabatta

Overview

Ciabatta (Italian: slipper bread) is a white Italian bread made with wheat flour, water, olive oil, salt, and yeast. Its hallmarks are an extremely open, irregular crumb dotted with large air pockets, a thin crisp crust, and a flat, elongated shape that resembles an old Italian slipper — hence the name. Despite its rustic appearance and ancient-feeling character, ciabatta is a relatively modern invention, created to compete with the baguette's rising popularity in Italy.

Origin & Country

Country: Italy
Region of Origin: Veneto (Adria, Province of Rovigo)
Creator: Arnaldo Cavallari, a baker and miller, who developed the recipe in 1982.

Ciabatta was born in the Veneto region of northern Italy. Arnaldo Cavallari, working at his family bakery, spent months experimenting to create a bread with a softer, more open crumb than existing Italian loaves — one that could absorb olive oil and fillings without becoming soggy.

History & Evolution

Year Event
1982 Arnaldo Cavallari invents ciabatta in Adria, Veneto
1985 Recipe trademarked; licensing begins across Italy
1985–1990 Spread to bakeries across northern and central Italy
1990s Introduced to UK, USA, and wider Europe
2000s Becomes the default bread for panini sandwiches globally

The bread's rapid popularization was partly commercial: Cavallari licensed the name and recipe to around 2,000 bakeries across Italy. By the 1990s it had crossed to the UK and North America, where it became synonymous with upscale sandwiches and restaurant bread baskets.

The key technical breakthrough was the use of a very wet dough (biga starter + high hydration), which produces the signature open crumb. Ciabatta dough is so wet it cannot be kneaded by hand and must be stretched and folded rather than shaped conventionally.

Recipe

Ingredients

Biga (Pre-ferment) — make 12–16 h ahead

Ingredient Amount
Bread flour (high protein) 200 g
Water (room temp) 130 ml
Instant yeast 0.1 g (tiny pinch)

Main Dough

Ingredient Amount
Bread flour 300 g
Water (warm, ~28 °C) 270 ml
Instant yeast 5 g
Fine sea salt 10 g
Extra-virgin olive oil 25 ml
All of the biga —

Method

  1. Make the biga: Mix flour, water, and yeast until shaggy. Cover and ferment at room temperature for 12–16 hours until bubbly and at least doubled.

  2. Autolyse: Combine biga, main flour, and 220 ml of the water. Mix until just combined. Rest 30 minutes.

  3. Add yeast & salt: Dissolve yeast in remaining 50 ml water; add to dough along with salt and olive oil. Mix vigorously with dough hook (or by hand using the "slap and fold" method) for 8–10 minutes until dough is smooth but still very tacky.

  4. Bulk fermentation (stretch & fold): Transfer dough to oiled container. Perform 4 rounds of stretch-and-fold every 30 minutes. Total bulk: 2–2.5 hours at 24 °C until dough has puffed and feels airy.

  5. Divide & shape: Generously flour work surface. Tip dough gently — do NOT degas. Cut into 2 rectangles. Stretch each gently to slipper shape (~30 × 10 cm). Dust heavily with flour.

  6. Final proof: Rest on floured couche or parchment for 45–60 minutes.

  7. Bake: Preheat oven to 240 °C (465 °F) with a baking stone or steel and steam pan. Slide ciabattas in. Add 1 cup boiling water to steam pan. Bake 20–22 minutes until deep golden and hollow-sounding when tapped. Cool on wire rack for 20 minutes before cutting.

Baker's percentages: 100 % flour, 80 % hydration, 2 % salt, 1 % yeast, 5 % olive oil.

Ciabatta con rosmarino Fresh rosemary folded into dough
Ciabattina Miniature rolls for individual panini
Frozen par-baked Industrial version sold globally in supermarkets

Cultural Context

Ciabatta arrived at a moment of anxiety in Italian baking: baguettes imported from France were displacing traditional Italian loaves in the 1970s–80s. Cavallari's creation was an explicit counter-move — an Italian bread that matched the baguette's lightness and versatility while showcasing olive oil, a quintessentially Italian ingredient.

Today ciabatta is Italy's most internationally recognized bread, appearing on restaurant menus worldwide and forming the foundation of the modern panini (pressed sandwich). In Italy itself, it remains a staple of the tavola calda (fast-lunch counters) and home baking.
