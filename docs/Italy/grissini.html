Grissini

Overview

Grissini (singular: grissino), also known as breadsticks or grissini torinesi, are slender, pencil-thin sticks of crisp, dry baked bread that have become an icon of Italian dining. Originating in the city of Turin in the Piedmont region of northwestern Italy, grissini are typically served as an antipasto in restaurants, often alongside or in place of conventional bread. Their distinctive crunch, light texture, and elegant elongated form make them equally suited to fine dining and casual snacking, and they pair famously with cured meats such as prosciutto.

Origin & Country

  • Country of origin: Italy
  • Region: Piedmont (Piemonte)
  • City: Turin (Torino)
  • Local name (Piedmontese): ghërsin

Grissini are deeply tied to Piedmontese culinary identity and are often labeled grissini torinesi ("breadsticks of Turin") to denote authenticity.

History & Evolution

The origins of grissini date to the 17th century. The earliest documented reference appears before 1643, when a Florentine abbot described a long, "bone-thin" bread being baked in Lanzo Torinese, a town just outside Turin.

According to tradition, the breadstick was invented in Piedmont by a Turinese baker named Antonio Brunero. The story holds that Brunero developed the thin, fully-baked bread for the young Duke Victor Amadeus II of Savoy, who suffered from digestive ailments in childhood. Because grissini are baked dry all the way through, with very little moist crumb, they were considered easier to digest than ordinary loaves — making them a medicinal as well as culinary innovation.

From these noble beginnings, grissini spread throughout Italy and eventually around the world. Notably, Napoleon Bonaparte is said to have been so fond of them that he had a regular supply sent from Turin to Paris, calling them les petits bâtons de Turin ("the little sticks of Turin").

In modern times, grissini have evolved into many forms:
- Italy: retained their classic thin, crisp character, often hand-rolled (stirati) and served with antipasti.
- North America: transformed into a softer, larger product, frequently brushed with butter, garlic, and cheese, or sweetened with cinnamon and sugar for dessert.
- Supermarkets worldwide: dried, pre-packaged grissini are sold as cracker-like snacks.

Recipe

Ingredients

For approximately 30–40 traditional grissini:

Ingredient Quantity
Type "00" or all-purpose flour 500 g
Warm water 280 ml
Extra-virgin olive oil 40 ml
Fresh yeast (or 7 g dry yeast) 15 g
Fine sea salt 10 g
Malt syrup or sugar 1 tsp
Semolina or extra flour (for dusting) as needed

Optional toppings: sesame seeds, poppy seeds, rosemary, coarse salt, cracked pepper.

Method

  1. Activate the yeast. Dissolve the yeast and malt (or sugar) in the warm water; let stand 5–10 minutes until foamy.
  2. Mix the dough. Combine flour and salt in a large bowl. Add the yeast mixture and olive oil. Mix until a shaggy dough forms.
  3. Knead. Turn out onto a lightly floured surface and knead for 8–10 minutes until smooth and elastic.
  4. First rise. Place dough in an oiled bowl, cover, and let rise at room temperature for about 1 hour, or until roughly doubled.
  5. Shape into a slab. Gently flatten the dough into a rectangle about 1.5 cm thick on a lightly oiled surface, brush the top with olive oil, and let rest for 20 minutes.
  6. Cut and stretch. Using a sharp knife or dough cutter, slice strips about 1–1.5 cm wide. Pick up each strip by the ends and gently stretch to the length of your baking sheet (this is the traditional stirati method).
  7. Top (optional). Roll the stretched sticks in seeds, herbs, or coarse salt if desired.
  8. Bake. Place on parchment-lined trays, leaving small gaps between sticks. Bake at 200 °C (390 °F) for 15–20 minutes, until deeply golden and fully crisp through.
  9. Cool. Let cool completely on a rack — this final drying step is what gives grissini their signature snap. Store in an airtight container.

Variations

  • Grissini Stirati: the traditional hand-pulled, irregularly shaped variety from Turin, prized for its rustic, knobbly texture.
  • Grissini Rubatà: a thicker, knotted-looking variant from the Chieri area near Turin, hand-rolled rather than stretched, with a denser bite.
  • Grissini al Sesamo / Papavero: topped with sesame or poppy seeds.
  • Grissini al Rosmarino: flavored with fresh rosemary and olive oil.
  • Grissini Integrali: made with whole-wheat flour for a nuttier flavor.
  • American-style soft breadsticks: thicker and pliable, often brushed with garlic butter and Parmesan, served with marinara or cheese dips.
  • Sweet dessert breadsticks: dusted with cinnamon-sugar or drizzled with icing — a North American innovation.

Cultural Context

In Italy, grissini occupy a place of quiet honor at the dining table. They are typically presented in a tall glass or paper sleeve at the start of a meal, often before the menu even arrives, signaling Italian hospitality. In Piedmont especially, they are a point of regional pride and a protected element of local food heritage.

A classic pairing is grissini wrapped in prosciutto, an antipasto fixture in trattorias from Turin to New York. They also accompany cheese boards, salumi platters, and aperitivo spreads with wines like Barbera, Nebbiolo, or Prosecco.

In North America, grissini have largely been reimagined as a casual, soft, buttery side item — popularized by chain Italian-American restaurants — and are frequently served with dipping sauces, in contrast to the dry, crunchy original meant to be eaten on its own.
