Baguette

Overview

The baguette (French: "wand" or "stick") is a long, thin loaf of French bread distinguished by its crispy golden crust, soft honeycomb interior, and iconic shape. Made from just four ingredients — flour, water, salt, and yeast — it exemplifies the French belief that perfect bread requires quality ingredients and careful technique rather than complexity. A UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage since 2022, the baguette is France's national bread and one of the world's most recognized food symbols.

Origin & Country

Country: France
City: Paris
Legal Definition: Defined by French law (Décret Pain, 1993) — must be made on-site from flour, water, salt, and yeast only
UNESCO Status: Inscribed on Intangible Cultural Heritage list, November 2022

History & Evolution

Period Event
1839 August Zang opens a Viennese bakery in Paris, introducing steam-baked bread
1920 French labor law bans bakers from working before 4 AM — thin loaves that bake quickly gain favor
1920s–1930s The elongated baguette shape becomes standardized in Paris
1993 French government issues the Décret Pain protecting traditional baguette ingredients
1994 Paris city government organizes the first annual Grand Prix de la Baguette
2022 UNESCO inscribes French baguette craft on Intangible Cultural Heritage list

The 1920 labor law is often cited as the practical reason baguettes became so long and thin: bakers could not start work before 4 AM but had to supply morning customers. A thin baguette bakes in 15–20 minutes versus 45+ minutes for a round loaf, solving the timing problem.

Recipe

Ingredients

(Traditional French bakery method — makes 3 baguettes)

Ingredient Amount
French Type 65 flour (or bread flour) 500 g
Water (cold, 4–8 °C) 350 ml
Instant yeast 4 g
Fine sea salt 10 g

Method

  1. Mix: Combine flour, yeast, and 320 ml cold water. Mix until shaggy (do not over-mix). Rest 30 minutes (autolyse).

  2. Add salt: Dissolve salt in remaining 30 ml water. Fold into dough over 3–4 minutes.

  3. Bulk fermentation: Ferment at 24 °C for 90 minutes with 3 sets of stretch-and-fold every 30 minutes. Then refrigerate 12–16 hours (cold retard builds flavor).

  4. Divide & preshape: Remove from fridge. Divide into 3 equal pieces (~285 g each). Gently preshape into oblongs. Rest 20 min.

  5. Shape baguette:
    - Pat dough into rough rectangle.
    - Fold top third down, bottom third up (letter fold).
    - Roll gently to extend to ~40–50 cm, applying even pressure.
    - Seal the seam with the heel of your hand.

  6. Final proof: Place seam-side down on a linen couche (or baguette pan). Proof 45–60 minutes at room temperature.

  7. Score: Using a lame (razor blade at 30° angle), make 5–7 overlapping diagonal slashes. Work decisively — do not drag.

  8. Bake:
    - Preheat oven to 250 °C (480 °F) with baking stone.
    - Inject or pour water to create steam (crucial for crust).
    - Slide baguettes onto stone. Bake 18–22 minutes until deep golden.
    - Cool on wire rack 10 minutes before eating.

Hydration: 70 % | Crumb: Open, irregular | Crust: Shattering crisp

Variations

Variation Description
Baguette de tradition Made per the 1993 decree; no additives; superior flavor
Baguette ordinaire Standard commercial version; often made with improvers
Pain au levain Sourdough version; more complex flavor; longer shelf life
Demi-baguette Half-length; sold individually or for smaller households
Ficelle Thinner, crispier; even faster bake
Flûte Between ficelle and baguette in size
Baguette viennoise Enriched with milk and sugar; softer crust

Cultural Context

In France, buying a daily baguette is not grocery shopping — it is a cultural ritual. The French consume approximately 30 million baguettes per day, with most purchased fresh from the local boulangerie and eaten the same day. A baguette tucked under the arm is a cultural image recognizable worldwide.

The Grand Prix de la Baguette de Paris is held annually to crown the best baguette in the city. The winning baker earns the right to supply bread to the Élysée Palace (the French presidential residence) for a year.

The baguette's simplicity is also its paradox: four ingredients, no fat, no eggs — yet infinite variation in outcome depending on flour quality, fermentation time, shaping skill, and oven temperature. Parisian bakers speak of the baguette the way sommeliers speak of wine.
