# Chapati

## Overview

Chapati is an unleavened whole-wheat flatbread that serves as a daily staple across the Indian subcontinent and much of East Africa. Made from just three core ingredients—whole-wheat flour (atta), water, and sometimes a pinch of salt—it is rolled into thin discs and cooked on a hot iron griddle called a *tava*, where it puffs dramatically over direct flame. Soft, pliable, and lightly toasted, chapati is the edible utensil of countless meals: torn into pieces to scoop up dal, sabzi, or stew, or rolled into wraps. It is a form of [[roti]], and the two terms are often used interchangeably.

## Origin & Country

Chapati originates from the **Indian subcontinent**, with deep roots in present-day India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka. From its homeland it has traveled widely:

- **East Africa** (Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania) — introduced by Indian traders and indentured railway workers
- **The Maldives**, where it is known as *roṣi* (or *foḷi* in the southern atolls)
- **Mauritius**, where the word entered Mauritian Creole
- **The global South Asian diaspora**, from the UK to the Caribbean

## History & Evolution

Evidence of chapati-like breads dates back to the **Indus Valley Civilization**, where wheat cultivation and griddle-baking were already well established. The bread is recorded in **Sanskrit literature** and features prominently in **Mughal-era texts**, where it accompanied richer courtly cuisine.

The very name reveals its method: *chapati* derives from the Sanskrit चर्पटी (*charpaṭī*), itself rooted in *charpa* meaning "flat"—a reference to the traditional technique of shaping dough by slapping it between the palms.

During the **British colonial period**, chapatis were widely consumed by both native Indians and Anglo-Indians, and the spellings *chewpatty* and *chowpatty* appear in British-Indian records. Chapati famously played a symbolic role in the mysterious "Chapati Movement" of 1857, when chapatis were passed from village to village in northern India shortly before the Indian Rebellion.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, **Indian indentured laborers**—particularly those building the East African railways—carried chapati to Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania, where it was enthusiastically adopted. East African chapati is typically richer and often shallow-fried rather than dry-griddled.

In the 21st century, urban Indian consumers have increasingly supplemented chapati with Western-style sliced bread, though it remains the foundational bread of home cooking. Industrially packaged chapatis are now sold widely as convenience food.

## Recipe

### Ingredients

For approximately 8 chapatis:

| Ingredient | Quantity | Notes |
|------------|----------|-------|
| Atta (whole-wheat flour) | 2 cups (240 g) | Stone-ground; 80–90% extraction is traditional |
| Water | 3/4 to 1 cup (180–240 ml) | Roughly 60–80% hydration relative to flour |
| Salt | 1/2 tsp (optional) | About 0.5–2% of flour weight |
| Oil or ghee | 1–2 tsp (optional) | For softer dough; some traditions omit |
| Extra atta | For dusting | |

### Method

1. **Make the dough.** In a wide bowl, combine atta and salt. Add water gradually, mixing with your fingers until a shaggy mass forms. Knead for 6–8 minutes into a smooth, soft, slightly tacky dough. If using oil, knead it in at the end.
2. **Rest.** Cover with a damp cloth and rest for 20–30 minutes. This relaxes the gluten and makes rolling easier.
3. **Divide.** Punch down and divide into 8 equal balls (about 40 g each). Keep covered.
4. **Roll.** Flatten a ball, dust with flour, and roll on a board into a thin disc roughly 6–7 inches (15–18 cm) across. Aim for even thickness; uneven discs will not puff.
5. **Heat the tava.** Place a flat iron tava or heavy skillet over medium-high heat until very hot.
6. **First side.** Lay the chapati on the dry tava. Cook for 20–30 seconds, until small bubbles appear on the surface.
7. **Second side.** Flip and cook another 30–45 seconds, until pale brown spots form.
8. **Puff.** Either flip again and gently press the edges with a folded cloth, *or* lift with tongs and place directly over an open gas flame for a few seconds. The chapati should balloon dramatically as steam inflates the layers.
9. **Finish.** Transfer to a cloth-lined basket. Optionally brush with ghee. Stack and cover to keep soft.

> **Tip:** Chapatis stale quickly—serve immediately, ideally hot off the tava.

## Variations

- **East African chapati** — Thicker, richer, and shallow-fried in oil rather than dry-cooked. Often slightly layered, similar to [[paratha]].
- **Rumali roti** — A paper-thin, handkerchief-like version cooked over an inverted convex griddle.
- **Phulka** — A smaller, thinner cousin always finished over open flame to puff fully.
- **Jowar / bajra / ragi rotla** — Made with sorghum, pearl millet, or finger millet flour; common in Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Karnataka.
- **Makki di roti** — A Punjabi winter favorite made from cornmeal (maize flour).
- **Rice and amaranth chapatis** — Gluten-free regional adaptations.
- **Maldivian roṣi / foḷi** — A localized version eaten with mas huni (tuna and coconut).
- **Rolex and kikomando** — Ugandan street foods built on chapati: *rolex* is a chapati rolled around an omelet ("rolled eggs"), while *kikomando* features chapati cut into strips and tossed with beans.

## Cultural Context

Chapati is far more than sustenance—it is the rhythmic backbone of South Asian domestic life. In countless households, the sound of dough being slapped and the smell of toasting wheat mark the start of the evening meal. It is traditionally made fresh for each meal, often by women of the household, and served straight from the tava to the plate.

In a typical thali, chapati is the **edible utensil**: a torn piece pinched around dal, vegetables, or curry replaces fork and spoon. This tactile relationship with food is central to Indian eating culture.

Religiously and socially, chapati is humble, democratic, and universal—eaten by every caste, class, and faith across the subcontinent. It appears in Sikh *langars* (community kitchens), Hindu temple offerings, Muslim *iftar* tables, and Christian Indian homes alike.

In East Africa, chapati has been so thoroughly naturalized that many Kenyans and Ugandans consider it a national dish. The word itself has been borrowed into **Swahili** and **Mauritian Creole**, evidence of its journey along the Indian Ocean trade routes.
