# Focaccia

## Overview
Focaccia is a flat, leavened, oven-baked Italian bread celebrated for its golden crust, tender crumb, and generous use of olive oil. Recognizable by the characteristic "dimples" pressed into its surface, focaccia occupies a central place in Italian baking — eaten as breakfast, table bread, antipasto, snack, or sandwich base. It is similar to the Roman flatbread [[pizza bianca]] and is sometimes considered a cousin of [[pizza]], though unlike pizza, focaccia is allowed to rise after being shaped before baking.

## Origin & Country
**Country:** Italy
**Region most associated with it:** Liguria (especially Genoa)

While variations exist across the entire Italian peninsula, focaccia is most closely associated with Ligurian cuisine. Outside Liguria, the unqualified word "focaccia" almost always refers to the Genoese variant.

## History & Evolution
The bread's lineage reaches back to antiquity. In ancient Rome, *panis focacius* was a flatbread baked directly on the hearth — the very name derives from the Latin *focus*, meaning "hearth" or "place for baking." Some food historians trace the basic recipe even further back, suggesting Etruscan origins.

The first written attestation of the word *focaccia* itself dates to the 14th century. Over the centuries, the bread became deeply tied to Ligurian baking culture, where Genoa elevated it into the iconic dimpled, olive-oil-glossed loaf known worldwide today. Regional adaptations followed wherever the bread traveled, evolving into sweet versions, stuffed versions, and potato-enriched versions, each shaped by local ingredients and traditions.

## Recipe
The following is a classic **focaccia genovese** recipe, the benchmark version of the bread.

### Ingredients
**For the dough:**
- 500 g (about 4 cups) bread flour or "00" flour
- 325 ml (1⅓ cups) lukewarm water
- 7 g (1 packet / 2¼ tsp) active dry yeast
- 10 g (2 tsp) fine salt
- 30 ml (2 tbsp) extra-virgin olive oil
- 1 tsp sugar or malt (to feed the yeast)

**For the topping (the "brine" and finish):**
- 50 ml (3½ tbsp) extra-virgin olive oil
- 50 ml (3½ tbsp) water
- 1 tsp coarse sea salt (flaky)
- Optional: fresh rosemary leaves

### Method
1. **Activate the yeast.** Dissolve the yeast and sugar in the lukewarm water; let stand 5–10 minutes until foamy.
2. **Mix the dough.** Combine the flour and salt in a large bowl. Add the yeast mixture and olive oil. Mix until a shaggy dough forms, then knead for 8–10 minutes until smooth and elastic.
3. **First rise.** Place the dough in an oiled bowl, cover, and let rise at room temperature until doubled (about 1½–2 hours).
4. **Shape.** Generously oil a rectangular baking pan (about 30×40 cm). Transfer the dough and gently stretch it to fill the pan. If it resists, let it rest 10 minutes and try again.
5. **Second rise.** Cover and let rise another 30–45 minutes, until puffy.
6. **Dimple.** Using oiled fingertips, press deep "dimples" all over the surface — these are the *ombrisalli* characteristic of Genoese focaccia.
7. **Brine.** Whisk together the olive oil, water, and a pinch of fine salt to form an emulsion. Pour over the dough so the dimples fill with the mixture. Sprinkle with coarse salt and rosemary if using.
8. **Final rest.** Let sit 15–20 more minutes while the oven preheats to 220 °C (425 °F).
9. **Bake** for 20–25 minutes, until golden brown on top and lightly crisp at the edges.
10. **Cool briefly** on a rack, then serve warm. In Genoa, locals traditionally enjoy it dipped in milk or cappuccino at breakfast.

## Variations
Focaccia is one of the most regionally diverse breads in Italy:

| Variation | Region | Defining Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Focaccia genovese** | Liguria (Genoa) | Dimpled, olive oil + coarse salt; the archetype |
| **Focaccia secca** | Liguria | Thin, biscuit-hard, dry version |
| **Focaccia di Voltri** | Liguria (Voltri) | Soft, oily, made with corn flour |
| **Focaccia di Recco** (con il formaggio) | Liguria (Recco) | Paper-thin dough sandwiching molten *prescinsêua* cheese; unleavened |
| **Focaccia dolce** | Northwest Italy | Sweet; sprinkled with sugar, sometimes raisins or honey |
| **Focaccia veneta** | Northeast Italy (Veneto) | Easter bread enriched with eggs, sugar, butter |
| **Piada dei morti** | Rimini | Sweet focaccia with raisins, almonds, walnuts, pine nuts; eaten on All Souls' Day |
| **Focaccia pugliese** | Apulia | Potato in the dough; topped with tomatoes, olives, oregano |
| **Osterfochaz / Fochiz** | South Tyrol, Krimml (Austria) | Easter focaccia with a thinner center to hold dyed eggs |
| **Focaccia al rosmarino** | Pan-Italian | Topped with fresh or dried rosemary |

## Cultural Context
Focaccia is far more than a bread — it's a daily ritual, especially in Liguria. In Genoa, it is eaten warm in the morning, often dipped in milk or cappuccino, a habit that surprises many visitors but is fiercely defended by locals. Throughout the day it appears as a snack, a table bread alongside meals, an antipasto, or a base for sandwiches.

Beyond Liguria, focaccia plays important ceremonial roles. In the Veneto, focaccia veneta is a centerpiece of Easter celebrations. In South Tyrol and parts of Austria, *Osterfochaz* is the traditional Easter gift from godparents to godchildren, with dyed eggs nestled into a dimple at its center. In Rimini, *piada dei morti* is baked in November to honor the dead on All Souls' Day. In Puglia, the potato-enriched focaccia pugliese is a staple of bakeries and family tables alike.

The continuity from Roman *panis focacius* to today's olive-oil-slicked loaves makes focaccia one of the most enduring living links between ancient and modern Mediterranean foodways.
