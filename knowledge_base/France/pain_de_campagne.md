# Pain de Campagne

## Overview
Pain de campagne — literally "country bread" — is a quintessential French rustic loaf, often referred to in English as "French sourdough." Traditionally shaped as a large round *miche*, it is made from a blend of white wheat flour with whole wheat and/or rye flour, water, leavening (natural sourdough or baker's yeast), and salt. Its hearty crumb, thick crust, and tangy, complex flavor make it one of the most beloved artisan breads of France.

## Origin & Country
**Country of origin:** France

Pain de campagne emerged from the rural baking traditions of French villages, where communal ovens served the entire community. Unlike the refined, urban [[baguette]], it represents the agricultural and peasant heritage of the French countryside, where bread was baked infrequently in large quantities to last a household for days or even weeks.

## History & Evolution
For centuries, French villages relied on shared communal ovens. Townsfolk prepared dough at home and brought it to the village oven for baking, producing massive *miches* weighing between 1.5 and 5.5 kilograms (3–12 lb). These loaves needed to keep well, which informed the dense, sourdough-leavened style.

Before the advent of roller milling in the late 19th century, nearly all wheat was stone-ground. To achieve a lighter loaf, millers and bakers sifted (or "bolted") the whole wheat flour through mesh or cloth, producing a whiter flour that still retained some of the bran and germ — a key contributor to the bread's character. Rye often found its way into the loaf because rye plants grew among wheat fields; up to 10% of the harvested grain could be rye. Since rye ferments faster than wheat and lends a distinctive earthy tang, this "accidental" inclusion became a defining trait.

The rise of commercial yeast at the turn of the 20th century pushed pain de campagne out of fashion in French cities, where the lighter, faster [[baguette]] took its place. The bread experienced a renaissance during the **artisan bread revival of the 1970s**, championed by bakers seeking a return to traditional methods, long fermentation, and natural leavening. Today, pain de campagne is once again a staple of French *boulangeries* and has gained enthusiastic followings in the United States and United Kingdom.

## Recipe

### Ingredients
*Makes one large miche (approx. 1 kg / 2.2 lb)*

| Ingredient | Quantity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Bread flour (white) | 400 g | High-protein |
| Whole wheat flour | 75 g | Stone-ground if possible |
| Rye flour | 25 g | Adds tang and complexity |
| Water (lukewarm) | 375 g | ~75% hydration |
| Active sourdough starter | 100 g | Or 5 g instant yeast |
| Fine sea salt | 10 g | |

### Method
1. **Mix the dough.** Combine the flours and water in a large bowl. Let rest (autolyse) for 30–60 minutes.
2. **Add leavening and salt.** Incorporate the sourdough starter (or yeast) and the salt. Mix thoroughly until smooth and cohesive.
3. **Bulk fermentation.** Cover and let ferment at room temperature for 4–6 hours (or longer if cool), performing a series of stretch-and-folds in the first 2 hours. The dough should grow noticeably and become airy.
4. **Shape.** Turn the dough out and shape into a tight round (boule).
5. **Proof.** Place seam-side up in a flour-dusted, linen-lined basket called a **banneton**. Cover and proof for 2–4 hours at room temperature, or overnight in the refrigerator for deeper flavor.
6. **Preheat oven.** Heat to 230 °C (450 °F) with a Dutch oven or baking stone inside.
7. **Bake.** Invert the loaf onto a peel or into the Dutch oven, score decoratively across the top, and bake for about **50 minutes**, until the crust is deep mahogany and the internal temperature reaches 96 °C (205 °F). Use steam in the first 20 minutes for a crisp crust.
8. **Cool.** Let the bread rest on a wire rack for at least 1 hour before slicing — the crumb continues to set as it cools.

## Variations
- **Pain de campagne au levain** — Made strictly with natural sourdough starter for maximum tang and shelf life.
- **Pain au seigle** — A higher-rye variation, sometimes 30–50% rye flour, with a denser crumb (see [[pain de seigle]]).
- **Pain complet** — A wholemeal version using primarily whole wheat flour.
- **Miche Poilâne** — The famed Parisian variant from the [[Poilâne]] bakery: a massive 2 kg stone-ground sourdough miche, scored with a distinctive "P".
- **Tourte de meule** — A regional cousin made with stone-ground (*meule*) flour, often even more rustic.
- **Pain de campagne aux noix** — Studded with walnuts; a popular cheese-board pairing.

## Cultural Context
Pain de campagne occupies a special place in French culinary identity as a symbol of rural authenticity and the slow-food ethos. While the [[baguette]] represents urban Parisian life, the miche speaks of farmhouse tables, family meals, and continuity with pre-industrial baking traditions.

The bread is a staple at French bistros and brasseries, where it is sliced thick and served alongside *charcuterie*, *pâté*, *rillettes*, soups, and aged cheeses. Its robust flavor and sturdy crumb make it ideal for *tartines* (open-faced sandwiches) and for sopping up rich sauces. A stale loaf finds new life in *pain perdu* (French toast) or country-style bread soups.

The artisan bread movement that began in 1970s France — and the parallel movement in the United States led by bakers inspired by figures like Lionel Poilâne — restored pain de campagne to its rightful place as a cornerstone of serious bread baking.
